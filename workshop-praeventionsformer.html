Contraception & Responsibility Workshop

Participatory Design · User Research · School Workshops · 2026

About

This workshop was developed as part of our research into contraception, gender and responsibility for the Future Consultation installation at Steno Museum.

Working directly with young people allowed us to explore what they already knew about contraception, how they understood responsibility, and how they responded to situations where familiar gender roles were challenged.

The workshop

We designed and facilitated a series of participatory activities with students from different school classes.

Through visual mapping exercises, contraceptive method cards and discussion-based scenarios, participants were invited to share their knowledge, assumptions and opinions without being presented with a predetermined correct answer.

The workshop format made it possible to observe not only what participants knew, but also how they negotiated responsibility and reacted to each other's perspectives in conversation.

Facilitation

Our role was not only to design the workshop materials, but also to facilitate the sessions and create a setting where sensitive topics could be discussed openly.

We guided the activities, introduced the scenarios, observed group discussions and adapted our facilitation when participants responded with humour, uncertainty or disagreement.

Because contraception can be both personal and socially sensitive, the workshop was designed to encourage participation without requiring students to disclose personal experiences.

Research insights

The workshops revealed clear differences in participants' familiarity with contraceptive methods. Condoms and birth control pills were widely recognised, while methods such as the vaginal ring, contraceptive patch, diaphragm and female condom were far less familiar.

The scenario discussions also gave us insight into how responsibility for contraception was understood. These conversations became an important foundation for the design of the Future Consultation and its deliberate use of mismatched contraceptive recommendations.

From research to design

Rather than treating the workshops as a separate research activity, the findings were directly incorporated into the design process.

Participants' responses helped us refine the scenarios, understand which contraceptive methods required additional context, and identify the assumptions around gender and responsibility that the final installation could meaningfully challenge.
